# Transcript P4 — Oceanside Loan Servicing, Repayment Plan Comparison Study

Session: 60 min scheduled, ran about 55, remote video call, screen-share
Participant: P4 — 61, retired high school physics teacher, about $19,000 remaining (graduate coursework loans), Oceanside customer for fourteen years
Moderator: M

---

## Part 1: Background

**M:** Okay, we're recording. So, thanks for, um, joining. Could you start by just telling me about your loans?

**P4:** Oh, gladly, gladly. So I went back for a master's in my late forties, because the district paid a bump for it, and, well, that was the theory. The bump was real but it was smaller than the brochure said, which is a whole other story. Anyway. I borrowed around thirty-four thousand, it's down to, I want to say nineteen, nineteen and change, and I'm on the ten-year, well, I was on the ten-year, now I'm on I think year eleven of a twelve-year-something because I had a deferment in there when my wife was sick. And I'll tell you, the thing I learned from all that, the thing that I think about constantly, is that nobody should ever look at the monthly payment. Nobody. It's the wrong number.

**M:** The wrong number?

**P4:** The wrong number. It's a trap, frankly. I'm not being dramatic. The monthly payment is the thing the whole system is designed to make you stare at, and the moment you stare at it you've lost. You should look at the total and the date and that is all. Payment's not information, it's a mood.

**M:** Ha. Okay. We'll, we'll come back to that, I'm sure. How often do you use the Oceanside site?

**P4:** Oh, regularly. I'm retired, I've got time. I log in probably once a month, I like to watch the principal line. It's a little hobby of mine. I keep a chart, in fact, I have a little spreadsheet, and I plot what the balance should be, against what it is. Physics teacher. You can take the man out of the classroom.

**M:** Nice. And have you compared plans before?

**P4:** Many times. Many, many times. I've compared them probably five or six times over the years, and every single time I end up at the same conclusion, which is, it's a bad idea to choose by monthly payment. Every single time.

**M:** Alright. So, let me get you into the task. If you share your screen, I'd like you to go to the Compare Plans page, and imagine you were deciding which plan to go with.

**P4:** Delighted.

---

## Part 2: Screen-share task

[P4 shares screen. Browser is maximized. He has a second spreadsheet window open behind it. He clicks "Repayment" then "Compare Plans" without hesitation.]

**P4:** I know my way around. So here's the table. And already, see, the first column after the name is monthly payment. They've put monthly payment first. Which is precisely the problem. The eye goes left to right and the first number you see is the smallest, friendliest number. It anchors you. That's not an accident, I don't think. I'm not accusing anyone, I'm a retired teacher, I'm not here to accuse people, but I've taught the anchoring effect to sixteen-year-olds, I know it when I see it.

**M:** So what, uh, what would you do with the order?

**P4:** Reverse it. Put the total cost first. Or, honestly, hide the monthly column until the person asks for it. "Show me my monthly payment." A little click. Make them want it. Because a person who's made to look at total cost first is a person who makes a better choice. I'd bet you a good dinner.

[P4 scrolls to the income estimator. Clicks the field, types 41000 (a pension figure), selects household size 2. Clicks "Update estimate." He watches the table update.]

**P4:** Good. I like this, this is useful. Now the income-linked one changed. See, but it's still leading with the payment, it says a hundred and twelve dollars. Lovely. A hundred and twelve. And the total at the end is nineteen thousand something, versus, well, I'm not going to pay nineteen thousand because I've only got nineteen thousand left. Hold on. Wait.

[P4 stops. Leans toward the screen. Hovers over the Income-Linked Flex "Total estimated paid" cell, then the Standard row total.]

**P4:** The Standard total says twenty-one thousand. And I owe nineteen. So the interest is two thousand. And the income one says twenty-six. So that one costs me five thousand more. For a payment that's, what, a hundred forty dollars cheaper per month. Five thousand dollars to save a hundred forty a month. Do you see? You could sell me a hundred forty a month and I'd hand you five thousand without noticing, that's what the monthly does, it hypnotizes.

**M:** Okay, so, what, uh, what do you look at first on this page, is what I wanted to ask.

**P4:** I look at the end date and the total. Always. The payoff date. Then the total. The monthly I look at only last, and only to make sure I can survive it. And I'd say most people do it backwards. Which is the whole point of what I'm saying.

[P4 hovers over the "i" tooltip beside Total estimated paid. Reads. Moves to the tooltip on "Payoff date." There is none. He makes a small noise.]

**P4:** There's no explanation of the date. Is that assuming I pay on time? Is it assuming I make every payment? That's one of those things, you need the assumptions. But, anyway, that's a minor point. The major point is, payment-first is upside down.

**M:** Right. I want to make sure, um, so you'd say the main issue is the order of the columns?

**P4:** The order, and the existence. Honestly the existence. If it were up to me, and I know it isn't, the monthly figure wouldn't be in the table at all. There'd be a second screen, after you've looked at everything else. The monthly payment is, it's dessert. You don't put dessert on the first page of the menu.

[P4 scrolls back to the top and clicks on the "Payoff date" header. Nothing happens. He clicks again, then says aloud that he assumed it would sort.]

**P4:** Nothing. Okay. I'd have liked to sort by payoff date. Actually that's a fine thing to offer. Sort by date, and you'd be nudging people to think about time. The date is the honest number.

**M:** Why the honest number?

**P4:** Because you can't fake a date. A payment, they can shape it any way they like. Forty years at fifty dollars a month, they'll call that affordable. But a date is a date. 2043 is 2043. And you know, when I see 2043, for a man my age, I think, I won't be here. You see what I mean? The date does something the dollar amount never does.

[P4 scrolls down. Looks at the Extended 25 row. Shakes his head.]

**P4:** Extended 25. No. They should probably remove that plan. Not because it's bad, just because it's a siren song. Everyone who picks that one is going to regret it by year twelve.

**M:** Do you think people should be nudged away from certain plans?

**P4:** I think they should be shown the consequence first. That's not nudging, that's education.

**M:** Okay. Um. Right, so, which one would you pick?

**P4:** I'd stay where I am. I can afford it. And that's my point too, really, I can afford it because I never let the monthly payment talk me into anything. When I went through the deferment, I was told "don't worry about the payment, it's only this much," and I remember thinking, well, that's the thing they want me to worry about least, so that's exactly what I should worry about most. Which sounds backwards but it isn't.

[P4 clicks "Select plan" on the Standard row. Reads the confirmation page. Says it is "very clear, actually." Clicks "Back."]

---

## Part 3: Follow-up

**M:** So, um, one more thing. You mentioned the monthly payment a lot. Did anything else on the page stand out?

**P4:** The income box. Wonderful, that was a good feature. I'd put it above the table, not below. And the tooltip on total, fine. But back to the thing, I can't stress this enough. If you want people to make better decisions, you'd lead with total cost and date. Monthly payment second or third, or even, hidden. The hiding is the important part. I've given this more thought than is healthy.

**M:** And the numbers, do you trust them?

**P4:** I do, because I checked. I've got my own projection on my spreadsheet and the Standard matches within a couple of dollars. That's how I tell. If it matches my math, I believe the rest. If you can't check it, you can't trust it. That's general. That's also why I want the assumptions listed. Anything that can't be checked is, I'm afraid, marketing.

**M:** Do you think a guided recommender would be helpful? Like the tool suggesting a plan?

**P4:** Oh, that'd be a mistake, unless it was a recommender that didn't mention monthly payment at all. If it started with "what payment can you afford," it'd be the same trap with a friendlier face. No. If they're going to build one, it should ask you "when do you want to be done?" That's the first question. When do you want to be free? Then it works backwards. The date is the question. And then afterward, only afterward, the payment is whatever it is.

**M:** That's a really interesting idea.

**P4:** It's not original. I stole it from how we plan a semester. You start at the last day and you count backward. You don't start with Monday.

**M:** Ha, yeah. Anything else?

**P4:** One thing, and then I'll stop. I know I've been going on. Everybody I've ever spoken to about this, my brother-in-law, my neighbor, the woman at church, they all say the same thing, "what's my payment?" And I say, that's the wrong question. Every time. It's like asking a runner how they feel instead of what their time was. So. Hide the monthly. That's my whole contribution to the field.

**M:** Noted. Thank you, P4, genuinely, this was great.

**P4:** My pleasure. You have my email if you have follow-ups. I do love a follow-up.

[Session ends. Total time approximately 52 minutes.]
